# Coding scheme

## How marking is recorded

Marking is recorded as **fixed labels in a spreadsheet**, not as free-text comments in a
word processor.

The reason is not convenience. Free text has to be interpreted before it can be counted,
and interpreting several thousand comments at scale would mean using a language model to
score a test of language models. Fixed labels are countable at the point of entry. The
workbook carries drop-down validation, so an invalid code cannot be entered in the first
place; every failure mode that a parser would have to guess at is prevented instead.

The workbook is generated by the analysis tool, not written by hand. The tool splits each
answer into sentences and numbers them, so the evaluators label the tool's units. This is
what allows the two halves of the measurement — human judgement and machine counting — to
be joined on a single key:

    T{repetition}-A{tool}-S{question}    e.g. T2-A1-S1

The `T` component numbers the repetition within its round, not a separate test: the instrument
is one test, applied twice per round. Workbook and raw-file names follow the same convention.

## Who marks

Two evaluators mark **by consensus**: they read each answer together, discuss it, and
record one agreed label. This is stated plainly because it has a consequence — inter-rater
agreement cannot be computed from a single agreed judgement. Consensus coding is a
recognised method, but it is not two independent ratings and is not reported as such.

Consensus is not a convenience here; it follows from the pair's competences. Neither
evaluator is a native speaker of both languages involved. Authority over the target
language rests with the native speaker of Adyghe; the Turkish side — the reference
translation for question 4 and the checking of Turkish wording — rests with the other
evaluator. Decisions about translation consistency are therefore reached by discussion
rather than assigned to one marker, and every label is recorded as the pair's joint
judgement.

With consensus coding there is no second, independent label sequence, so inter-rater
agreement cannot be computed. What can be measured instead is the pair's own stability over
time, by re-marking later from a clean file. **This was left outside the pilot's scope**
(decision of 2026-09-17): the pilot's priority was settling the instrument itself. It is
adopted as a requirement for non-pilot runs — with different texts and conversation patterns,
the evaluators re-mark **the whole set** a week later, from a clean file. Marking the whole
set rather than a sample avoids any question about how the sample was drawn, and is the only
way the rarer codes occur often enough to say anything about their stability.

## Units of analysis

Three linguistic levels, three different units. Each level is judged at the unit where it
actually lives:

| Level | Unit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Syntax | sentence | Word order and clause construction are properties of a sentence. |
| Morphology and lexis | word | A wrong affix or an invented word is a property of a word. |
| Semantic adequacy | whole answer | Whether the answer meets the question is a property of the answer. |

## Sentence level — every sentence is labelled

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `C1` | correct |
| `C2` | faulty but comprehensible — construction is wrong, the meaning still comes through |
| `C3` | faulty and incomprehensible — no meaning comes through, or the opposite meaning does |

The middle category carries most of the information. For a low-resource language it
separates a model that has the system and slipped from one that does not have the system
at all.

## Word level — only faulty words are recorded

Correct words are not labelled. The denominator comes from the tool's word count, so the
number of correct words is the total minus the number recorded here.

**Word count is computed from whitespace-separated items; an item with no letter in it at
all — punctuation standing alone, a dash, an emoji, a bare digit run — is not counted as a
word.** A letter elsewhere in the same item (attached punctuation, an emoji stuck to a real
word) does not change anything; only an item with *no* letter anywhere in it is excluded.

| Code | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `FRM` | wrong form — the word exists, its inflection or affix is wrong | wrong case marking |
| `CHO` | wrong choice — the word is well formed but is the wrong word here | «нысащэ» where «джэгу» is meant |
| `INV` | invented — no such word exists in the language, **and** the form cannot be read as an adaptation of a loan from anywhere; this includes a form assembled from real morphemes in a combination the language does not license | «искусственнай», which is neither Russian nor Adyghe |
| `MIX` | code mixing — a word from another language, in that language's own form | `severek`, `день рождения` |
| `TRM` | term choice — **not an error**; an established loan used where a native term covering the same referent exists | «стол» where «Ӏанэ» covers the same table |

The codes are abbreviations of their English roots: `FRM` form, `CHO` choice, `INV` invented,
`MIX` mixing, `TRM` term (see the version note below for the earlier, Turkish-rooted codes).

**The boundary between `FRM` and `INV`** is whether a correct form of the word exists at all.
If a correct form exists and only the inflection or affix attached to it is wrong, the word is
`FRM`. If the word itself — root and affixes combined — does not exist in the language, it is
`INV`, even when every individual morpheme is real and the combination merely looks plausible.

**Each word is counted under exactly one code.** Counting a word under two categories at once
is not a harmless double bookkeeping entry: it silently breaks two checks downstream — the
correct-word count and the contact-language-total-equals-`MIX` check both go wrong with no
visible cause.

**The table above is a reference list, not the procedure.** The procedure is the five-question
decision table in the workbook's Info sheet, which the evaluators read while marking. That table
asks its questions in the order `FRM` · `CHO` · `MIX` · `INV` · `TRM`, taking the first "yes";
the reference list above is ordered `FRM` · `CHO` · `INV` · `MIX` · `TRM`. The two orders were
allowed to diverge because each is right for its own purpose, and they agree on every word
provided `INV` carries the loan-adaptation clause it is given above — «искусственнай» is `INV`
under either order because it cannot be read as an adaptation of a loan, and «информация» is
`MIX` under either order because it can. Revision 3 dropped that clause on the reasoning that
the row order made it redundant; it did not, because the order is not the same in both documents.
Revision 4 restores it, which makes the answer independent of which of the two tables is read.

`MIX` carries a second field, the **contact language**: a word count for each of a closed set
of four — `KBD`, `RUS`, `TUR`, `ENG` — plus a single total word count for every other language
combined. No language name is recorded for that last one; if it matters which language it was,
that goes in the free-text note instead, but the count itself is what is counted. The four are
counted separately because they are different phenomena. Kabardian mixing into Adyghe means the
model cannot keep the two Circassian languages apart — a central question of this study. Russian
mixing is contact influence. Turkish mixing, in this instrument, follows a task that required
Turkish, and so measures failure to return to the target language. The combined "other" total
exists because a tool can mix in a language none of the four anticipates (Spanish, observed in
practice); a closed list would either lose that finding or push it into free-text notes, where
it cannot be counted. Because these are a breakdown of `MIX` by source, the four plus "other"
must sum to `MIX`'s own count for that sentence — a mismatch means a word was missed or double-counted.
This breakdown is for `MIX` only: a word counted as `TRM` is never also counted into a contact-language
field, even though many `TRM`-coded words are themselves loans with an obvious source language
(«стол» is Russian) — the temptation to mark that source is exactly the
trap, since `TRM` never enters `MIX`'s count and so has no business in its breakdown either.

**The boundary between an established loan and code mixing** is adaptation. A word of
Russian origin that has been adapted to the language's morphology (`искусственнэ`) is
part of the lexicon and is not an error. The same word left in its source form
(`искусственный`) is `MIX`. A form that is standard in neither language
(`искусственнай`) is `INV`.

**A loan enters the language together with a referent, and is an Adyghe word only within it.**
«стол» came in for the four-legged table, the traditional «Ӏанэ» having three legs; «студент»
came in for the university student, where Russian splits what Adyghe covers with one word.
Used for that referent, such a word is an Adyghe lexical item and the `TRM` question applies to
it. Used for any other referent it has not entered the language at all and is foreign material:
«студент» for a secondary-school pupil is `MIX`, not `CHO`, because the pupil is «еджакӏо» in
Adyghe and «ученик» in Russian — the word is outside the referent it was borrowed for, so it is
not an Adyghe word being misused, it is a Russian word being reached for. Lexicalisation attaches
to a form together with its meaning, never to the form alone.

**Whether a word is in the language is decided by currency of use, not by region.** A word that
has entered Adyghe and is in active use is an Adyghe word wherever it is spoken. No speaker of
any language knows every word of it, so a speaker who does not recognise «стол» does not thereby
put it outside the lexicon. What does vary by region is which referents a native term covers, and
that is what decides `TRM`:

    university student, in Russia,  «студент»    «еджакӏо» does not cover that referent there
                                                 → the right word; no code
    university student, in Turkey,  «студент»    «еджакӏо» does cover it → TRM
    university student,             «еджакӏо»    the native term, deliberately → no code
    secondary-school pupil,         «студент»    outside the loan's referent → MIX

**Whether a bare, unaffixed loan is `TRM` or `MIX` is decided from context, not from the word.**
Affixed forms — «столым», «столкӏэ» — are Adyghe. A bare form is judged by whether the syntactic
slot required an affix:

    тхапэ горэ столе телъ.    the slot required «столым»; a Russian form stands there  → MIX
    мыр стол.                 the slot licenses a bare form                            → TRM

No sentence arrives on its own: there is always the question it answers, or the text around it.
The evaluators judge from that context, and the judgement is a joint one (see "Who marks"). A
rule this context-sensitive is only applicable because two evaluators must agree on it; it could
not be handed to a single marker working alone, and it could not be mechanised.

**A foreign-language gloss the tool supplies for its own word is not coded.** When a tool writes
an Adyghe term and follows it with a parenthetical equivalent in another language — «имафэ дахэ
(день рождения)», «инысащэ (свадьба)» — the parenthetical is not part of the answer; it is the
tool commenting on its own answer, signalling that it is unsure of the term it chose. It is
counted under no code: not `MIX`, not `TRM`, not `INV`. It is still counted as a word, because it
is in the text.

The gloss may, however, be read as evidence when judging the Adyghe term itself: it shows what
the tool meant, which is sometimes the only way to decide whether the term it chose was the right
one. A marking in round 1 records exactly this use. This holds whatever the gloss's language or
script, including a Turkish gloss written in Cyrillic; the script of such a gloss remains within
scope of the character-level analysis, which is independent of word coding.

`TRM` is counted separately from the error codes. It measures **term usage preference**, not
competence — and not, as an earlier version of this document claimed, "the reach of terminological
planning": there is no terminological planning for Adyghe whose reach could be measured. What is
observable is narrower and more concrete, namely which of two available terms the tool chose. Correspondingly, the correct-word count is derived only from the
error codes — `FRM`, `CHO`, `INV`, `MIX` — and never has `TRM` subtracted from it: a term-choice
word is a correctly formed word, so counting it against "correct" would contradict the very
reason `TRM` exists as a separate code.

**A sentence marked `C1` (correct) cannot contain a word counted under an error code**
(`FRM`, `CHO`, `INV` or `MIX`). The reverse does not hold: a sentence whose every word is
correct can still be `C2` or `C3` on word order alone, since syntax is a property of the
sentence, not of its words (see "Units of analysis" above). `TRM` is outside this rule — a
`C1` sentence may still contain a term choice.

### Why only faulty words

Across the pilot's four repetitions the material comes to roughly 4,250 words but only about 600 sentences in
the target language. Labelling every word would mean several thousand separate judgements,
which invites fatigue error — and fatigue error is not random, it accumulates towards the
end. Labelling every sentence is tractable; recording only the faulty words within those
sentences costs almost nothing extra, because the evaluator is already reading them.

The cost of this choice is that an overlooked word counts as correct. A per-answer
**Marked** flag closes most of that gap by separating "no errors found" from "not yet
examined", which is why that flag is mandatory.

## Answer level

| Field | Values |
|---|---|
| Language compliance | `TAM` in the target language · `KARISIK` mixed · `BASKA` in another language |
| Semantic adequacy | `KARSILADI` met · `KISMEN` partly met · `ALAKASIZ` unrelated |
| Task-specific reading | questions 5 and 7 only — see below |
| Marked | `EVET` · `HAYIR` — mandatory |

Where language compliance is `BASKA`, the sentence and word labels are left empty:
labelling a text that is not in the target language against that language's categories
would be meaningless. This value is not hypothetical — in round 1 one tool answered the
first question with 173 words of English metalinguistic commentary instead of answering it.

Two questions carry a reading of their own, because the seven questions are not
homogeneous and a single semantic scale would miss the copying probe entirely.

| Question | Values | Recorded by |
|---|---|---|
| 5 — translation back, **copying probe** | `OZGUN_GERI_VERDI` returned the original wording — copied · `DEGISIKLIGI_YANSITTI` reflected the deliberate change — processed · `BELIRSIZ` | the tool |
| 7 — difference between the two paragraphs | `FARKI_DOGRU_BULDU` processed both texts · `FARK_YOK_DEDI` did not align them · `FARKI_YANLIS_BULDU` reported a difference that is not there | an evaluator |

**Question 4 is not scored for translation accuracy.** What this instrument measures is
Adyghe conversational competence, not translation quality: every question is a request
expressed in Adyghe, and what is judged is whether the tool understood the request and
attempted to meet it. A wrong translation is still a translation and shows the request was
understood; a Turkish answer to a Turkish paragraph would show it was not. Question 4 is
therefore covered by language compliance and semantic adequacy like any other answer.

In question 5 the Turkish paragraph differs from the Adyghe original of question 4 in one
deliberate place. A tool that returns that place in its original form has copied rather
than processed.

## Character analysis — done by the tool, no judgement involved

**Scope rule.** Checks specific to the Adyghe writing system — palochka, alphabet
inventory, Cyrillic/Latin homoglyphs — run **only inside Cyrillic-script tokens.** They are
not applied to Latin text. The reason is concrete: question 4 asks for a translation into
Turkish, where `l` and `ı` are ordinary letters. Applied to a whole answer, the look-alike
check produced more than fifty false hits per Turkish answer. Scope is part of the method,
not an implementation detail.

The mixed-script check is language-independent and runs on every token, because a Turkish
word written with a Cyrillic letter can appear inside an Adyghe answer.

Measured for every answer:

- **Palochka accuracy** — counts of U+04C0 and U+04CF against look-alikes typed in their
  place (U+0049 `I`, U+006C `l`, U+007C `|`, U+0031 `1`, U+0406, U+0456, U+0131, U+0399,
  U+03B9, U+01C0, U+00A6), within Cyrillic tokens only.
- **Mixed-script words**, at two severities. A Latin proper noun with a Cyrillic affix
  (`Anthropic-м`) is legitimate and recorded as such; writing systems interleaved inside
  one morpheme (`дüğün`) is an error.
- **Characters outside the alphabet inventory**, taken from the published alphabet
  resource rather than from a list kept in the code.
- **Unicode normalisation form.** NFC is required. The same letter can be encoded more
  than one way — `й` as U+0439, or as U+0438 followed by U+0306 — and the two are visually
  identical but not equal. Decomposed input makes counting, searching and comparison fail
  silently. Palochka is unaffected: U+04C0 and U+04CF have no decomposition.
- **Invisible characters** (Unicode category `Cf`).
- **Counts**: characters, words, sentences; mean sentence and word length; type/token
  ratio.
- **Similarity to a reference text**, for question 5.

Repair precedes counting. A palochka typed as `|` splits a word in two for any tokeniser
and inflates the word count, so look-alikes are normalised on a working copy before
anything is counted. The original text is never modified, and every repair is listed.

The similarity column is read in reverse: an independent translation is equivalent in
meaning but different in wording. Near-identity is a reason for suspicion, not a mark of
success. It is interpreted together with the question 7 answer.

## Why the evaluation is not blind

Blinding was considered and deliberately not applied. At A2–B1 level the criteria are
largely objective — is the word spelled correctly, is the sentence well formed, is the
meaning consistent — and the character counts come from a tool rather than from judgement.
Beyond that, question 1 asks the tool to introduce itself, so some tools name themselves in
their answers; hiding identity would have meant altering the very text being marked. Output
length and formatting habits also differ enough between tools that blinding would not have
held in practice. Claiming blinding on paper while not achieving it is worse than
evaluating openly.

One compensating measure is applied: marking proceeds question by question rather than tool
by tool. Re-marking to measure the pair's stability over time was left outside this pilot's
scope (decision of 2026-09-17); see the note on evaluator reliability below.

Blinding will be reconsidered for a scaled study.

### Revision 3 — 2026-09-17

The decision table's `INV` question dropped its second sentence ("does such a word not
exist in any language?"). It asked evaluators to verify something no one can verify, and it
was redundant besides: the table is read top to bottom and `MIX` is already checked before
`INV` (see revision 1 below), so by the time a word reaches the `INV` row it has already failed
the `MIX` test — the row order alone carries the distinction the sentence was trying to add
in words. No coding category's scope changed; this is a decision-table wording fix, found while
the instrument was being trialled — before marking began — not a new or removed category.

**Evaluator reliability was taken out of the pilot's scope.** Up to this date the scheme
listed re-marking a subset as a compensating measure for the absence of blinding. It was not
carried out and will not be: the pilot's priority was settling the instrument, which was
itself revised three times (revisions 1–3) before any answer was coded. The thesis reports this
as a limitation. For non-pilot runs the measure is adopted as a requirement, and in a stronger
form than before — **the whole set**, not a subset, re-marked a week later from a clean file.
Marking everything removes any question about how a sample was drawn, and is the only way the
rarer codes occur often enough for their stability to be measurable at all.

Separately, worth recording as a detail of the instrument's usability rather than a coding
change: the decision table's five questions were rewritten in Turkish, English and Russian,
and the Russian text was reviewed and approved line by line by the study's Russian-reading
evaluator before marking began.

### Revision 2 — 2026-09-16

Found while trialling the instrument, before marking began: a response containing an emoji
had its word count inflated by one, because the tool counted every whitespace-separated item
as a word, including ones with no letter in them at all (an emoji on its own, a lone dash, a bare
